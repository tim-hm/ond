# Breathing foundations evidence ledger

This ledger is the source check behind the citation-free copy on **The basics**. It is deliberately stricter than the screen: a plausible mechanism is not a promised outcome, an immediate physiological change is not a lasting benefit, and one technique outperforming another is not assumed unless a head-to-head trial shows it.

The canonical wording and order live in [`crates/migrate/src/seed/catalogue.rs`](../../crates/migrate/src/seed/catalogue.rs). Keep this ledger in step whenever a retained claim changes.

Where a claim also appears in [breathing-science.md](breathing-science.md), that document carries the fuller citation — trial designs, sizes and the refusals around them — and this one carries the shipped boundary. A row that names a section there is pointing at the evidence rather than restating it.

| Practice matters more than perfect | Deliberate breathing has modest benefits; a repeatable, studied slow practice is a reasonable default. | [Fincham et al. meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/); [Balban et al. trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/9873947/) | The meta-analysis found no clear dose-response. | What should a good breath feel like? | Deep or fast overbreathing can produce air hunger, tingling and dizziness; reducing the breath or returning to normal is the conservative response. | [Merck Manual: Hyperventilation syndrome](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/pulmonary-disorders/hyperventilation-syndrome/hyperventilation-syndrome) | This is safety guidance grounded in respiratory physiology, not evidence that one comfortable depth produces better mental-health outcomes. Persistent or concerning symptoms are not reframed as a practice problem. |
| Is a deep breath always the answer? | No: when panic is rising, over-breathing is what produces the dizziness and air hunger, and smaller, slower breathing is the better direction. | breathing-science.md §6.1 carries the citations — the panic-hypocapnia account, the capnometry-assisted therapy trials, and the study where raising and lowering CO₂ worked equally well. | The mechanism is contested, so the copy hedges the outcome (“may help”), says outright that why it helps is still argued, and claims only the direction. The trialled therapy is clinician-led and capnometry-assisted, which the copy says plainly is not what this is: “none of this is treatment for panic”. The symptom pair is stated without becoming a checklist. |
| Why can slow breathing help? | Respiratory sinus arrhythmia makes heart rate rise during inhalation and fall during exhalation; slow breathing reliably increases immediate HRV-related measures. | [Shao et al. meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12671-023-02294-2) | The emotional finding was marginal and long-term effects were unclear. The copy promises a reliable cardiovascular change while practising, not that the body always settles before the mind. |
| Belly or chest? | The diaphragm is the primary muscle of quiet breathing, and abdominal movement is a useful way to notice it. | [Hopper et al. systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31436595/) | The review found only three eligible studies. The cue is optional; chest movement is normal and is not labelled a stress pattern. |
| Nose or mouth? | The habit is nasal breathing across the ordinary day, which a session practises. The nose filters, warms and humidifies inhaled air, and its resistance paces airflow. | [Nasal breathing evidence review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10013266/) | Whether it helps healthy people much is uncertain, and the copy says so — not merely that the magnitude is unknown. No oxygen-transfer claim, and mouth breathing stays explicitly permitted. Nasal nitric oxide is left to humming breath's mechanism prose (breathing-science.md §3.12), which carries it with the measured figure, rather than paid for twice on a ceilinged page. Mouth taping is refused and did not fit the budget: the boundary is nasal days, not taped nights, with the 2025 review and its numbers in §4.2. |
| How slow? | Five to six breaths per minute is a commonly studied slow pace. | [Shao et al. meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12671-023-02294-2); [400-person coherent-breathing trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10719279/) | A matched 12-breaths-per-minute practice performed similarly on mental-health outcomes in the 400-person trial. Five to six is presented as common, not universally optimal, and comfort outranks the count. |
| What about fast breathing and holds? | Fast breathing can lower carbon dioxide and cause tingling, dizziness or fainting; long holds can end in gasping. | [Merck Manual: Hyperventilation syndrome](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/pulmonary-disorders/hyperventilation-syndrome/hyperventilation-syndrome) | The page does not claim that an empty-lung hold is uniquely calming or trains carbon-dioxide tolerance. Both are optional and carry practical risk controls. |
| How should I get comfortable? | Posture and gaze are comfort and context choices, and attending to the breath is optional: following the on-screen guide rather than the feeling is a legitimate way to practise, as is stopping early. | No comparative outcome claim retained for posture. Safety and ease decide. The permission line names no population and makes no claim that an external pacer works better — it removes an implied requirement rather than adding an instruction. |
| How long and how often? | A single session may help acutely; many longer studies repeat short sessions over several weeks. | [Fincham et al. meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/); [Balban et al. trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/9873947/); [400-person coherent-breathing trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10719279/) | The optimal dose remains unknown. The copy does not assert a five-minute floor, a ten-to-twenty-minute plateau or that distributed practice must beat the same time in one sitting. |
| When breathing itself is the problem | Roughly one adult in ten cannot reliably get a satisfying breath despite healthy lungs, and the pattern has a name. | [Population prevalence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11337441/); breathing-science.md §6.7 for the retraining evidence and the constituency | The entry exists to name an experience somebody may not know is a known thing — the basics page is where they learn it exists, and the `when-you-cant-get-a-satisfying-breath` occasion is where they act on it. So the copy stops at recognition: no retraining explanation (the route carries it), no red-flag triage (the route's safety note carries it, at the moment it matters), no comorbidity named, and no symptom checklist that would work as a self-test. “Treatable” rests on the retraining review, which is consistently positive and consistently low quality; it is not a claim that this app is the treatment. |
| How good is the evidence? | Randomised trials show small-to-medium aggregate effects on stress and anxiety, with risk-of-bias and heterogeneity concerns; immediate cardiovascular findings are firmer than emotional or lasting outcomes; the largest blood-pressure effect in the literature belongs to a calibrated inspiratory resistance device rather than to a pacing app. | [Fincham et al. meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/); [Shao et al. meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12671-023-02294-2); [pace and ratio study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smi.3496); [IMST at −9 mmHg](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.121.020980), with breathing-science.md §6.6 for the surrounding nulls | A pooled benefit does not establish one ideal pace or ratio. The page calls the evidence promising, modest and uneven, and does not position breathwork as a replacement for treatment. The device sentence is a disclaimer — it exists to stop a reader crediting this app with the field's strongest number — and names the resistance device only, because the −9 mmHg result is that device's and nothing in the blood-pressure literature belongs to a capnometer. |
| Why doesn't önd score you? | Popular breathwork scores are not established proxies for broader health or life improvement. | [BOLT and exercise-performance study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11406178/); [400-person coherent-breathing trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10719279/) | A score may be repeatable or reflect an immediate physiological state without being a validated health outcome. önd records practice and self-comparison without turning either into a verdict. |

## Editorial guardrails

- Lead with the action somebody can take; put mechanism second and uncertainty last.
- Keep the lead answer to 60 words, ordinary answers to 55, the evidence answer to 90, and the complete page to 650 words. Seed tests enforce these ceilings. The page total binds first: a new topic is paid for out of the existing ones, and a claim that will not fit is dropped from the copy and held in the boundary column here rather than shipped over budget.
- Say “may” or “can” for emotional outcomes. Reserve “reliably” for immediate cardiovascular changes supported across studies.
- Treat nose breathing, abdominal movement, exact counts, posture and gaze as refinements rather than pass/fail rules. The nasal entry leads with the daily habit because that is where the physiology applies, not because the refinement was promoted to a requirement — mouth breathing stays permitted in the same answer.
- Treat fast breathing and holds as distinct, optional higher-care practices.
- Do not infer that every breathing technique is equivalent, that any practice works regardless of form, or that one ideal pattern has been established.
- Name no population and no syndrome. Where a topic exists to serve a group — breath-focus aversion, dysfunctional breathing — describe the experience and leave the label out.

## Presentation decision

The page carries no illustrations. The previous instructional vectors added height without making the guidance clearer, while generic lifestyle scenes would add atmosphere rather than instruction. Put the practice-first topic in an Overview section and give it the same question-and-answer hierarchy as every other topic.
